Basics of Selected Volatile Regime: Oxygenated Treatment (OT)

Specific chemical mechanics, dosing injection points, and supercritical boiler application details aligned with IAPWS TGD3-10(2015).

PREFERRED FOR SUPERCRITICAL
1. What is it?

Controlled addition of high-purity gaseous Oxygen (O2) (30 - 150 µg/kg) and small amounts of Ammonia (NH3) into ultra-pure feedwater (CACE < 0.15 µS/cm).

2Fe₃O₄ + ½ O₂ → 3 α-Fe₂O₃
2. Where is it dosed?

Oxygen injected at Condensate Polisher Outlet (CPO) or Deaerator Outlet / Feed Pump Inlet. Ammonia is injected at CPO.

NH₃ + H₂O ⇌ NH₄⁺ + OH⁻
3. How is it dosed?

High-purity medical grade Oxygen (99.99%) fed from gas cylinder racks using mass flow controllers into stainless steel spargers directly in the pipe center line.

Target DO: 30 - 150 µg/kg
4. Effect & Oxide Layer

Converts porous Magnetite into extremely dense, smooth Ferric Oxide Hydrate / Hematite (α-Fe2O3). Dissolution rate drops to < 0.5 µg/kg.

Fe Release < 0.5 µg/kg
Primary Application & Strict Limitations for Once-Through / Supercritical Boilers:

Standard regime for all modern supercritical once-through boilers with 100% condensate polishing and all-ferrous feedwater metallurgy. CRITICAL RULE: CACE MUST remain strictly < 0.15 µS/cm; if CACE exceeds 0.15 µS/cm, oxygen injection must immediately cease to avoid severe localized pitting.

Why is Hematite or Magnetite Preferred?

Reasoning from IAPWS TGD3-10(2015) Sections 3, 4, 6.2 and 6.3 — the preferred oxide depends on the feedwater metallurgy and the chemistry regime.

Reason for the selected regime:

Why Hematite / FeOOH film (OT, AVT(O)) is preferred on all-ferrous systems
  • Very low solubility: the ferric oxide/oxyhydroxide (FeOOH on magnetite, trending to α-Fe2O3) hardly dissolves in flowing high-purity water, so iron release falls to < 1 µg/kg (OT) or < 2 µg/kg (AVT(O)).
  • Protects the single-phase flow regions: the film passivates carbon steel feedwater piping, economizers and LP evaporators, the places where single-phase FAC has caused fatalities (Section 6.3).
  • Not strongly pH-dependent: in the absence of contaminant anions, corrosion is suppressed to a very low level across pH 7–10, so protection does not rely on high ammonia.
  • Less iron transport → cleaner boiler: fewer corrosion products reach the evaporator, reducing deposits, under-deposit corrosion (UDC) and boiler ΔP growth.
  • Condition: needs ultra-pure water (CACE < 0.15 µS/cm for OT) and all-ferrous feedwater metallurgy; stop O2 if CACE > 0.3 µS/cm (many plants use 0.2–0.25).
Why Magnetite (AVT(R)) is still used in some plants
  • Copper-alloy protection: mixed-metallurgy feedwater heaters need reducing conditions (AVT(R)) to limit copper corrosion and copper transport to the boiler and HP turbine (Table 2).
  • Magnetite is the natural base oxide on carbon steel (3Fe + 4H2O → Fe3O4 + 4H2) and is semi-protective, forming in any case beneath the ferric film.
  • Its solubility depends strongly on pH: raising pH (at 25 °C) from 8.7 to 9.6 lowers magnetite solubility at 200 °C about ten-fold, so the higher the pH the better.
  • But pH is capped at 9.1–9.3 with copper alloys, so magnetite protection is limited: feedwater Fe < 2 µg/kg is the optimum, and FAC risk remains higher.
  • Not suited to HRSGs / all-ferrous units: AVT(O) is recommended instead because of single-phase FAC concerns and Fe > 2 µg/kg.

Supercritical & Once-Through Unit Requirements

In once-through boilers, water is pumped through heating circuits in a single continuous pass without recirculation or steam drum separation.

Phase Transition Zone (PTZ) Risks

As supercritical fluid transitions from dense water-like state to steam-like fluid (around 374°C, 22.1 MPa), any dissolved salts (NaCl, Na2SO4) lose solubility instantly and precipitate onto tube walls. Maintaining CACE < 0.15 µS/cm prevents concentration of corrosive acids in the PTZ.

Condensate Polishing Unit (CPU) Mandate

100% deep-bed condensate polishing is mandatory for once-through units operating on Oxygenated Treatment. CPU removes ionic impurities and corrosion products continuously, safeguarding against sudden condenser tube leaks.

Boiler Differential Pressure (ΔP) Control

Under AVT(R), porous magnetite deposits form "ripple" topography on the inner tube wall under high water flow velocities (> 2 m/s). This increases hydraulic roughness, forcing plant derating due to high feed pump discharge pressure. OT forms smooth hematite, curing ripple growth.

Superheater Attemperation Spray Water

Spray water taken from feedwater for steam temperature control is injected directly into superheated steam. Any non-volatile chemical or salt present in feedwater passes directly into high-pressure turbine blades.

IAPWS TGD3-10 Glossary & Terms

Standard acronyms and technical definitions.

CACE (Conductivity After Cation Exchange) Also known as Cation Conductivity. Measures conductivity after sample passes through an acidic cation resin exchanger ($H^+$ form). Indicates presence of corrosive anions ($\text{Cl}^-$, $\text{SO}_4^{2-}$). OT Target: < 0.15 µS/cm.
OT (Oxygenated Treatment) Treatment regime using high-purity water, ammonia, and 30–150 µg/kg oxygen addition to establish a highly protective Hematite ($\alpha\text{-Fe}_2\text{O}_3$) oxide film.
AVT(R) (All-Volatile Treatment Reducing) Ammonia treatment with reducing agent (hydrazine) added to eliminate oxygen (< 5 µg/kg). Primary for mixed copper metallurgy systems.
AVT(O) (All-Volatile Treatment Oxidizing) Ammonia treatment without reducing agent, maintaining ambient dissolved oxygen between 3–30 µg/kg.
PTZ (Phase Transition Zone) The region in once-through boilers where liquid water transitions to steam. Highly vulnerable to impurity concentration.
FAC (Flow-Accelerated Corrosion) Dissolution of protective oxide layer caused by fluid flow turbulence, leading to sudden pipe wall thinning. Completely eliminated under OT.

Dosing in Demineralisation (DM) Plant

UF pretreatment and cleaning · RO membrane protection · ion-exchange regeneration

Theme:
Process overview

Why does a UF–RO based DM plant need chemicals?

Interactive learning module

A DM plant removes suspended particles, dissolved salts and residual impurities to produce low-conductivity make-up water. Chemicals are used at different stages for different jobs: hypochlorite and acid for UF cleaning sequences, antiscalant and antioxidant ahead of RO, and acid/caustic for ion-exchange regeneration. They are not interchangeable, and their dosing points matter.

1 · UF
Protect / clean membranes

Hypochlorite and acid injection points are shown at the UF inlet as shown in the process arrangement; actual use must follow the approved operating sequence.

2 · RO feed
Prevent scale

Antiscalant delays sparingly soluble salts from precipitating on the RO membrane surface.

3 · RO feed
Remove oxidant risk

Antioxidant/reducing agent is injected on the RO feed line before the cartridge-filter block to consume residual oxidant before it can damage sensitive RO membranes.

4 · Ion exchange
Restore resin capacity

HCl regenerates cation resin; NaOH regenerates anion resin. In addition, caustic dosing is shown at the RO permeate header for the specified permeate pH-conditioning duty.

Chemical 01 · UF inlet

Sodium hypochlorite (NaOCl) — Hypo

Oxidation of organic / biological foulants
Animated cells represent the cleaning mechanism
Dosing point & functionUF inlet, as shown in the process diagram. Hypo provides active chlorine species that oxidise organic deposits and help control biological fouling when included in an approved UF treatment or cleaning sequence.
Chemical equilibrium / reactionNaOCl → Na⁺ + OCl⁻
OCl⁻ + H₂O ⇌ HOCl + OH⁻
HOCl/OCl⁻ are the active chlorine species; their relative proportion depends strongly on pH.
Underdosing / no doseInsufficient oxidation can leave organic or biological deposits, increase UF fouling, and reduce permeability or cleaning effectiveness.
Overdosing / excess contactCan oxidatively damage membrane material, seals or other components and create unsafe residuals. Never let hypo carry into a chlorine-sensitive RO feed.
Parameters affecting doseOrganic load, biofouling level, pH, temperature, active-chlorine strength, contact time, membrane material/compatibility, cleaning sequence and rinse endpoint.
Chemical 02 · UF inlet / cleaning duty

Hydrochloric acid (HCl) — UF duty

Mineral deposit
Acid dissolves acid-soluble deposits
Cleaning depends on membrane-approved recipe
→
Dosing point & functionUF inlet connection shown in the process diagram. Acid is used only as specified in the UF treatment/cleaning sequence to remove acid-soluble mineral deposits; it is not automatically a continuous feed dose.
Representative reactionCaCO₃(s) + 2HCl → CaCl₂ + CO₂↑ + H₂O

UnderdosingAcid-soluble deposits may remain, so UF permeability may recover poorly after cleaning.
OverdosingLow pH, excessive concentration or contact time can damage membranes/metals, increase corrosion and create an unsafe neutralisation burden.

Chemical 03 · RO feed before cartridge filter

Antiscalant

Crystal growth / agglomeration inhibited
Threshold inhibition and dispersion
Dosing point & functionRO feed line upstream of the cartridge filter. Antiscalant delays nucleation/crystal growth and helps keep scale-forming material dispersed; it does not remove dissolved salts or replace RO rejection.
Chemical mechanismNo single stoichiometric reaction applies to commercial antiscalants. Adsorption/threshold inhibition delays crystal growth; dispersant groups can reduce particle agglomeration. Scale examples include Ca²⁺ + CO₃²⁻ → CaCO₃(s), which antiscalant is intended to inhibit.
UnderdosingGreater risk of carbonate/sulphate/silica-related scale, increasing normalized pressure drop and reducing RO performance.
OverdosingUnnecessary cost; product-specific overdosing or incompatibility may contribute to fouling or interact with other treatment chemicals.
Parameters affecting doseFeed-water analysis, hardness, alkalinity, sulphate, silica, pH, temperature, RO recovery, concentration factor, membrane flux, product chemistry and compatibility.
Chemical 04 · RO feed before cartridge filter

Antioxidant / reducing agent

Cl₂+→Cl⁻
Residual oxidant is reduced before reaching RO membrane
Dosing point & functionRO feed line upstream of the cartridge filter. A reducing agent (commonly sodium metabisulphite or another approved product) consumes residual oxidant that could damage chlorine-sensitive RO membranes.
Representative reactionFor bisulphite reacting with hypochlorous acid: HSO₃⁻ + HOCl → HSO₄⁻ + HCl
Exact reaction and dose basis depend on the selected reducing-agent product.
UnderdosingResidual oxidant may reach the RO membranes and cause irreversible oxidation damage, loss of rejection or shortened membrane life.
OverdosingExcess reducing agent can encourage biological growth in some systems, alter downstream chemistry and increase chemical consumption.
Parameters affecting doseMeasured free-chlorine/oxidant residual, flow, product active content, reaction/mixing time, pH, temperature, dosing-pump calibration and residual-monitor reliability.
Chemical 05 · RO feed before cartridge filter

Hydrochloric acid (HCl) — RO feed duty

HCO₃⁻+H⁺→CO₂ + H₂O
Acid reduces alkalinity / carbonate scaling tendency
Dosing point & functionRO feed line before the cartridge filter, as shown in the process arrangement. Where included in the operating philosophy, acid lowers pH/alkalinity and shifts bicarbonate toward dissolved CO₂, reducing carbonate-scale tendency. It must not be assumed necessary for every RO design.
Chemical reactionH⁺ + HCO₃⁻ → CO₂(aq) + H₂O
Acid does not remove total dissolved salts by itself; it changes carbonate equilibrium and may increase dissolved CO₂ load.
UnderdosingIf acid is required for the selected water chemistry, high pH/alkalinity may leave greater carbonate-scale risk.
OverdosingLow pH can increase corrosion risk, alter membrane feed conditions and increase downstream CO₂ or neutralisation demand.
Parameters affecting doseFeed alkalinity, pH, calcium, temperature, recovery, carbonate saturation, acid concentration, feed flow and RO membrane operating limits.
Chemical 06 · RO permeate header

Caustic soda (NaOH)

H⁺+OH⁻→H₂O
Neutralisation / pH adjustment of RO permeate
Dosing point & functionRO permeate header. NaOH raises pH when required by the downstream water-quality target. Separately, caustic is used for anion-exchange resin regeneration; that batch duty is not the same as permeate-header dosing.
Chemical reactionsH⁺ + OH⁻ → H₂O
For dissolved CO₂: CO₂ + OH⁻ → HCO₃⁻; with further alkalinity: HCO₃⁻ + OH⁻ → CO₃²⁻ + H₂O.
UnderdosingPermeate pH may remain below target, potentially affecting downstream corrosion control or process requirements.
OverdosingHigh pH can exceed downstream specifications, create caustic exposure hazards and increase carbonate precipitation risk where hardness is present.
Parameters affecting dosePermeate pH, dissolved CO₂, alkalinity, permeate flow, target pH, caustic concentration, mixing quality, downstream process requirements and analyser calibration.
